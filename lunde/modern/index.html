Overview and principles

Lundiyani is the modern form of Lunde: an isolating lingua franca spoken across the conworld, much later than Old Lundian and very different from it.

  • History. Many peoples speak it, so it has dialect and register doublets, borrowings, and changes from Old Lundian that aren't always regular. The imperfection is intended.
  • Type. Verbs never change form. Tense, aspect, mood and negation are separate particles. The only grammatical suffixes are possessive -de and the plurals -i, -en and -nen.
  • Etymology. Most modern words have no worked-out etymology, and none is needed.
  • Working method. New forms are checked against the rules below. Only contradictions with a stated rule and real ambiguities get flagged.
  • Sounds. There is no h sound; Old h is lost (Old hara → ara). x is a different sound.

The full word list is in the Lexicon.

  • -de attaches to any word, nouns included: i onde osili (the person's eyes).
  • -de is a remnant of the Old Lundian genitive (kide, ude, dade).
  • Each other: on-a-oni. ale lau on-a-oni (they love each other).
  • Reflexive: amon. a lo kanxi amon (she looked at herself).

Word order

The basic order is subject, verb, object, and modifiers come before what they modify.

  • Before the noun: articles, demonstratives, possessors and adjectives. nei kawe on (this tall person), ide kora (my cat).
  • Before the verb: negation and every tense, aspect or mood particle.
  • After the noun: relative clauses. i on xaon lo lai yada (the person who was singing).
  • At the end: the question particle me.
  • Prepositions are separate words. No word attaches to another except the suffix -de and the plural endings.
  • Exceptions: ta (to have) and commands put the verb before the subject.

  • lai is also the copula (i lo lai kili, I was young) and, at the start of a sentence, means “there is”: lai anei nai ci louba (there is water in the bottle); negated, lan lai anei (there’s no water).
  • lan comes after kiena and tefri, which work like adverbs: ale kiena lan lo eden (they haven’t eaten yet).
  • A modal or second verb takes the bare verb: i lan sa valia (I can’t sleep), ilie mail kapa (we need to talk).
  • Passive: tense + dei + verb, with amb for the agent. ci pay lo dei raxavi amb ni wakti (the village was destroyed by the warriors); i lo dei fastixi (I was blinded). dei comes from Old daze (by him or her), the agent in the Old passive of be + participle + ablative.

Nouns, articles and numbers

Plural nouns take -i, articles come from Old Lundian, and the numbers are the Old Lundian ones, unchanged.

  • Plural -i: osil → osili (eyes), kiwuru → kiwuri (dogs), kora → kori (cats), on → oni (people).
  • Inanimate nouns take -en after a consonant and -nen after a vowel: skin → skinen (books), louba → loubanen (bottles). Irregular: yami → yamo (feet). After any number above one the noun is plural: ra kiwuri (two dogs).
  • Articles: the Old Lundian set without its accents (table below). Many speakers also use i with inanimate nouns.
ArticleAnimateInanimatePlural
theicini
a, someiaanna

an (a) and an (to) share one form; context tells them apart.

  • Adjectives after lai: -de marks a permanent quality such as color or size, and only in a plain “X is ADJ” sentence: i onde osili lai lasode (the person’s eyes are blue). It never appears before a noun (ko kiwuru, a big dog) or in comparisons. Without -de the word names a state or an impression: i lai kuali, literally “I am happiness” (kuali = happy, happiness).
  • Comparison: kaw + adjective + da. i wuru lai kaw ko da i kiwuru (the wolf is bigger than the dog).
  • Superlative: i kaw + adjective. a lai i kaw leia on nai ci payi’an (she is the most beautiful person in the city).

Numbers count in base twelve, as in Old Lundian. Before a noun the bare form is used; when counting, é- goes in front (é-na, é-ra).

Questions

Yes/no questions end in me; question words stay where the answer would go, or move to the front.

  • o lo vera ne me? (Did you do it?)
  • o lai vera xa? or xa o lai vera? (What are you doing?) In place is more grammatical; both are common.
  • xaon lau o? (Who loves you?) A subject question word is already in place at the start.
  • Questions with a question word take no me.

Clauses and commands

Subordinate clauses open with a conjunction; relative clauses use sa after the noun; commands are ov + verb + person.

  • and: a before a consonant, ad before a vowel. ide kora ad ode kiwuru (my cat and your dog).
  • when: xanarya after the main clause: a lo lai yada xanarya o lo luran (she was singing when you came). When the when-clause comes first, xar, though xanarya also works: xar i lo lai kili, i talei lo yada (when I was young, I used to sing).
  • if: as, with lei in both clauses. as o lei luran, i lei lai kuali (if you came, I would be happy).
  • Other conjunctions: ema (because), ant (but), anta (before), nes (until), dal or ana (while), no (or).
  • Relative clauses: noun + sa + clause, as in Old Lundian, for people and things: i on sa yada lai ide bana (the person who sings is my brother). ci skin sa o lo sila lai leia (the book that you wrote is beautiful). xaon is also used for people: i on xaon lo lai yada lai kuali (the person who was singing is happy).
  • That-clauses: xa. i so xa o na luran (I know that you will come).
CommandMeaning
ov luago! (formally ov lua o)
ov lua ilielet's go
ov lua alet him or her go
ov lua alelet them go

The person comes after the verb. With singular you it has fallen out of use (ov eden, eat!), but formal writing keeps it (ov eden o). Negation follows ov: ov lan lua ilie (let’s not go). ov keeps commands apart from o (you).
